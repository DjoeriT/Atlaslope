Why Rioja tastes the way it does

Two climates that rarely meet share one sheltered valley. That is where Rioja's wines get their body, their colour and their acidity.

Pliego · pp. 15, 19Map tour4 minutes · 9 places to fly to
1The place

The place: A valley with walls

The vineyards follow the Ebro for 100 km, from Haro in the west to Alfaro in the east, in a valley never more than about 40 km wide. They climb in terraces to about 700 m. The mountains around it make Rioja a natural region of its own, and the Sierra de Cantabria to the north protects the vines.

Three provinces share the vineyard: La Rioja, Álava and Navarra, 63,593 hectares across 144 municipalities.

2The weather

The weather: Cool in the west, warm in the east

The whole valley sits where the Atlantic and the Mediterranean meet, so temperatures stay mild and about 400 mm of rain falls in a year. Moving east, the climate turns Mediterranean and the wine changes with it.

3The ground

The ground: Three soils, three weights

Clay-limestone covers a quarter of the vineyard, on terraces and small plots in Rioja Alavesa, the Sonsierra and parts of Rioja Alta. It is the main source of wines for ageing. Ferrous clay, red and rolling, covers another quarter. Alluvial soil near the rivers, flat, deep and full of stones, covers half. Both of these give wines with less body than the clay-limestone.

Most of Rioja's soil is balanced between sand, silt and clay, slightly alkaline, poor in organic matter, and holds a moderate amount of water through the summer.

4What the rules make

What the rules make: Made for the barrel

Wine is about a fifth of the region's economy, and Rioja's long focus on ageing has built one of the largest concentrations of oak barrels in the world. Crianza, Reserva and Gran Reserva are the result.

Rioja makes sparkling wine too. Wines "made by the Champagne method" from Rioja Alta and Alavesa were shown in Madrid in 1857, and in 1890 a French firm in Haro's station quarter hired a champagne maker from Reims. The cool end of the Winkler heat scale keeps the base wines fresh and acidic. Since 2017 the rules cover traditional-method sparkling whites and rosés.
